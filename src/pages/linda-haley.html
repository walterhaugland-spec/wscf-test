Her story

Classrooms, gardens, and kids who needed something to do

Linda spent [X years] as an educator [confirm: grades, schools, subjects, and any counseling or special-education roles]. In classrooms and school gardens, she kept seeing the same thing. When kids had real work in front of them, living things depending on them, and an adult who noticed what they did well, they settled. Worry quieted. Confidence showed up.

She carried that lesson home to her own garden and her own family, and it stayed with her: kids don't only need to be told they are capable. They need chances to prove it to themselves.

Why she started Warm Springs

A 100-year-old horse farm, put to work for kids

In 2018, the Haley family bought a horse farm on Warm Springs Avenue in Boise's East End. Horses and people had worked that land for more than a century. Linda wanted to keep that history and give it a new job.

[Linda's own account of the moment that started it. The draft placeholder is not her story.]

She founded Warm Springs Care Farm with licensed marriage and family therapist Amy Grace and parent advocate Deborah McFadyen. It began with a few animals, a handful of volunteers, and a small group of kids. Today the farm runs Barn Club, Pre-4-H Club, the Teen Leadership Crew, school field trips, and volunteer days, and hosts partner programs and farm-based therapy.

Building a model for other farms

What works here can work in your community

Warm Springs shows that a small working farm can support children's mental health, and that the approach can be taught. Linda wants every community with land, animals, and people who care to know they can build something like this.

She is documenting how the farm runs, from programs and volunteer training to animal care, safety, and clinical partnerships, so others can start with a proven plan instead of a blank page.
